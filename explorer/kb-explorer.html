What's in the knowledge base

A research library about the gadgets and apps used to collect health data from people — smartwatches, rings, chest straps, and the phone software that quietly records where someone goes and how much they sleep. It answers three separate questions, one per module. Everything below opens: click any row.

The short version

Anyone planning a health study has to pick their instruments. Hand out Fitbits or Oura rings? Can you get the raw heart-rate signal back out afterwards, or only a tidied-up "sleep score"? Will people still be wearing the thing in six months? Vendors answer the first two questions in marketing language and do not answer the third at all.

This knowledge base answers all three from original sources, and keeps the three kinds of answer deliberately apart.

Module 1 · 15 write-ups

The devices

Wrist and finger hardware — Apple Watch, Oura, WHOOP, Fitbit, Garmin, plus the research-grade kit. What each measures, and what you can actually get back out of it.

Module 2 · 10 write-ups

The phone platforms

Software that turns a participant's own phone into a sensor — location, movement, screen use — plus pop-up questionnaires. Who runs the servers, and who holds the data.

Module 3 · 65 write-ups

What actually happened

Real studies that deployed the things in Modules 1 and 2. How many signed up, how many stayed, what broke. The part no vendor publishes.

Module 4 · 48 papers

Methods and reviews

Reviews, consensus statements and position papers about how this research is done, filed regardless of platform. A filing home for now; no write-ups yet.

Modules 1 and 2 describe capability. Module 3 describes reality.

That split is the whole design. A vendor page will tell you a wristband streams skin-response data. Module 3 has a study where four hospitals used that exact wristband, and the sites streaming it live lost about half their data while the sites recording to the device's own memory lost under ten percent. Same hardware, same people wearing it, opposite outcome — decided by a settings choice nobody documents as important.

So Modules 1 and 2 never claim a device works. They record what it offers. Module 3 supplies the evidence about what happened, and the two link to each other instead of repeating.

What is a "write-up"?

The repository calls these . Rather than define the word, here is the actual skeleton every device and platform write-up follows — the same headings, in the same order, every time, so any two can be compared line by line.

Module 3 uses a different skeleton, because the thing being described is an event rather than a product.

Why the headings are fixed

If a fact isn't available the heading stays and the answer is "unknown". That sounds pedantic and it is the point: a missing row is visible, whereas a quietly dropped section looks like a finished document. Every claim also carries one of four confidence markers — — so nothing reads as more certain than its source supports.

Module 1 — the devices

Fifteen write-ups on wearable hardware and the software around it. The organising question is not "is this accurate" but what can a researcher actually collect, access, export and use, under what conditions, and at what cost.

Module 2 — the phone platforms

Ten write-ups on software: systems that collect from a participant's own smartphone in the background, and usually push questionnaires at them too. The question is what a team can collect, where the data lives, and how much infrastructure they have to run themselves.

Module 3 — what actually happened

Sixty-five real deployments, each read in full rather than skimmed from the abstract, and each summarised by the single most useful thing it teaches a team about to make the same choice. Filter by technology, or search for a problem you're worried about.

Module 4 — methods and reviews

48 papers that are about neither a device nor a deployment: reviews, consensus and ethics statements, validation frameworks, reporting guidelines and methods papers on missing data, adherence and equity. Ten came from a lab publication pull that turned out to be broader than Beiwe; the rest from a baseline search on 3 September 2026. Three further papers are catalogued but paywalled. The module has a catalogue and PDFs but no write-ups yet.

Keeping it current

Four shared documents hold everything not specific to one module, and an automated job runs every Monday to catch new papers.

The Monday job, in four steps

Step 1

New device papers

Searches the US National Library of Medicine's database for anything new on Oura, WHOOP and Apple Watch, and files each with a note on who funded it.

Step 2

New platform papers

The same per phone platform, across two databases — several of these teams publish in computing venues medical databases don't index. Free papers are downloaded and kept.

Step 3

Shortlist studies, write nothing

Module 3 candidates go on a to-read list. The job is forbidden from writing a study write-up itself.

Step 4

Log and save

One dated entry describing what the run found, then everything saved in a single batch.

Why step 3 refuses to write

Modules 1 and 2 catalogue papers about a technology, which can be done safely from an abstract. Module 3 asserts what happened during a deployment, which cannot — those numbers live in the full text, and abstract-level screening in this project got the platform wrong about three times in twelve. So the job shortlists and a person writes.

The publishing rule

Nothing written for other people to read goes anywhere public until the full text has been shown and approved with the exact phrase confirmed, post it. Data files, PDFs and bookkeeping updates are exempt; new prose is not. The Monday job is the single carve-out, since it runs unattended and can't ask — and in exchange it may only write factual run logs, into this one repository, notifying nobody.
