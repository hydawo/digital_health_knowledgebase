## 1. Moshe et al. 2021, Frontiers in Psychiatry

- File: `txt/2021-moshe-frontierspsychiatry-predicting-depression-anxiety.txt`
- First author (byline): Isaac Moshe. Year 2021. Journal: Frontiers in Psychiatry 12:625247. DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2021.625247. PMCID: not in PDF.
- Platform/device actually deployed (Methods, p.3-4): "participants were emailed an online link to download a custom smartphone application called 'Delphi' developed for Apple (iOS) smartphones. ... Delphi uses the AWARE open source framework (44, 45) to collect raw data from smartphone sensors. Sensors enabled in the current study included Battery, GPS, Screen (on and off), and Timezone. In addition, we used the ESM Scheduler plugin to deliver the EMAs." Wearable: "We used the Oura Ring to measure participants' activity, sleep and HRV." Ring generation is not stated. Filename suggests nothing about platform; the AWARE iOS framework is a Module 2 profiled platform (`aware-framework`) and Oura is Module 1, so criterion (b) is met on both counts. Note the iOS AWARE integration was custom (acknowledgement to Yuuki Nishiyama "with his support on the AWARE iOS framework integration").
- Verdict: BORDERLINE-include. It is a genuine 30-day multi-device deployment (AWARE-iOS + Oura + EMA) with a stated dropout breakdown and missingness rates, which is exactly the kind of combination Module 3 wants and which no current profile covers (all existing AWARE profiles are Android-era or Android-plus-iOS; none pair AWARE with Oura). It is borderline because the operational content is a single paragraph: no wear-time, no per-sensor completeness, no technical failure modes beyond GPS sensing errors, and the paper is primarily an analytic (prediction) paper.
- Existing Module 3 profile covering this cohort: none.

Extraction (INCLUDE-level)

- Design: "longitudinal observation study with repeated measurements over a 30-day period" (Study Design, p.3). Baseline day 1, midpoint day 16, endpoint day 31 DASS-21; EMA mood 3x/day.
- Funding/COI: Academy of Finland grants 316253, 320089, 318927 (DF, KO); NIMH P50 MH119029, R01 MH111610 (DM); Wihuri and Yrjö Jahnsson Foundations (LP-R). "The authors declare that the research was conducted in the absence of any commercial or financial relationships" (p.12). No Oura involvement stated.
- Population/setting/country: general-population adults, non-clinical, recruited online; "The majority of participants (66%) were from Finland" (Results, p.6); remainder other countries, not itemised. Ages 24-68, mean 42.8 (SD 11.6); 92.7% white; 80% bachelor's or higher. Conducted during first-wave COVID restrictions, April-May 2020.
- Enrolled to analysed: N = 60 at intake. Dropouts: "1 participant (1.7%) dropped out of the study due to concerns over privacy, 2 participants (3.4%) dropped out due to burden of self-report and 2 (3.4%) participants dropped out for unknown reasons. Of the remaining 55 participants, 47 (85.5%) completed the midpoint questionnaire and 54 (98.2%) completed the endpoint questionnaire" (Results, p.6). Analysed N = 55.
- Duration: 30 days per participant; recruitment 12-29 April 2020.
- Deployment model: BYOD on both instruments. Inclusion required that participants "(c) owned an iPhone with access to the internet, (d) owned an Oura Ring" (p.3). No devices provisioned.
- Custom integration: Delphi app wraps AWARE (Battery, GPS, Screen, Timezone sensors + ESM Scheduler plugin); Oura data pulled through Apple HealthKit ("Participants were required to provide Delphi with permission to access location data at all times, Apple Healthkit and enable notifications from the app", p.3). "Data collected by Delphi is first stored locally on the participant's device and then uploaded onto a secure server in the cloud when a WiFi connection is established" (p.4). Sampling frequencies are in Supplementary Table 1, not in the PDF.
- Monitoring: "a secure web-based dashboard was developed displaying the data gathered for all participants, updated at 15-min intervals. Cases of missing data were resolved via email. At the end of the study, participants were requested to uninstall the app" (p.3).
- Recruitment method: "posts on online communities and social media sites" (p.3). Over-recruited deliberately: target N = 40 per Saeb et al.; "recruitment was continued for 2 weeks after reaching the required sample size to compensate for dropout" (p.3). Compensation: a personalised mental-health report reviewed by a clinical psychologist; no payment.
- Retention definition and rate: not formally defined; 55/60 (91.7%) remained enrolled; questionnaire completion 85.5% (midpoint) and 98.2% (endpoint) of the 55.
- Attrition reasons: privacy concern (1), self-report burden (2), unknown (2).
- Data completeness: "Missingness only occurred in the DASS-21 assessment (10%) and the sensing variables (9.1%)" (Missing Data Handling, p.5). No per-sensor or per-day breakdown; no definition of what a missing sensing-day is. Multiple imputation (MICE, 20 datasets) used. GPS cleaning: removed duplicates, points above the 80th percentile of accuracy, and "GPS coordinates with latitude 0.0 and longitude 0.0 that arose due to sensing errors" (p.4).
- Wear-time definition: not reported.
- OS breakdown: iOS only by design ("the study was open only to participants with an Apple (iOS) smartphone", Limitations p.9); the authors flag this as a sampling bias.
- Documented technical failure modes: GPS zero-coordinate sensing errors (p.4). Nothing on battery, app crashes, upload gaps, HealthKit sync, or Oura sync.
- Authors' feasibility conclusions/recommendations: none framed as feasibility; recommendations are analytic (larger samples, longer periods, both OS platforms, clinical populations).
- NOT reported (a study designer would want): ring generation and firmware; wear-time or nights-with-sleep-data; per-stream completeness; battery impact of always-on GPS on iOS; whether the 9.1% sensing missingness was concentrated in particular participants; EMA response rate (only DASS completion is given); any device-level failure; how the "cases of missing data were resolved via email" worked or how often.

## 2. Mason et al. 2022, Scientific Reports (TemPredict parent study)

- File: `txt/2022-mason-scientificreports-tempredict-covid-detection.txt`
- First author (byline): Ashley E. Mason. Year 2022. Journal: Scientific Reports 12:3463. DOI 10.1038/s41598-022-07314-0. PMCID: not in PDF.
- Platform/device actually deployed (Methods "Oura ring data", p.10): "All participants wore the Oura Ring Gen2 (ouraring.com), a commercially available wearable sensor device (Oura Health, Oulu, Finland), on a finger of their choosing. The Oura Ring connects to the Oura App ... via Bluetooth." Data sharing via in-app opt-in: "opt to share their Oura data with UCSF (from within the Oura App)" (Procedures, p.9). Surveys on UCSF Qualtrics. No Module 2 platform. Matches filename.
- Verdict: BORDERLINE-include, as a companion to Shiba rather than a standalone. Reasoning: the paper is an algorithm paper, but it does report its own enrolment funnel at a scale (65k) that no Module 3 profile has, plus recruitment mechanics (in-app invitation from the vendor, site-by-site HCW enrolment with mailed sizing kits), the provisioning numbers, the data-quality inclusion thresholds that reduced 306 confirmed cases to 73 usable ones, a non-wear detection rule, and vendor data-access restrictions. It does not report adherence, wear-time, completeness distribution, or attrition reasons, so on its own it would be "N enrolled, N analysed"-plus. Paired with Shiba it gives the parent-study context that Shiba explicitly defers to.
- Same cohort as Shiba? Yes, nested. Shiba's N=100 FAU healthcare workers are a subset of the 3,180 study-provisioned HCW arm described here (FAU is listed among the sites; Shiba: "a subset of participants within the larger University of California, San Francisco, TemPredict Study"). Each adds distinct operational figures: Mason adds the enrolment funnel, recruitment mechanism, provisioning count, data-quality selection thresholds, non-wear rule, funding/hardware from Oura, and data-sharing policy; Shiba adds per-night wear adherence and per-day survey adherence with subgroup breakdowns for one provisioned site. Recommendation: one Module 3 profile keyed on Shiba (the adherence paper), with a "Parent study" section drawing the funnel and deployment-model facts from Mason, and Mason cross-linked in Module 1's Oura profile as the algorithm paper. If two profiles are preferred, Mason's should be labelled as funnel-only.
- Existing Module 3 profile: none.

Extraction (INCLUDE-level, deployment content only)

- Design: prospective observational cohort with daily symptom surveys and monthly surveys; retrospective algorithm development (Discussion: "we developed this algorithm using retrospective approaches, and testing feasibility and utility of real-time deployment is a key next step", p.8).
- Funding/COI (p.14): USAMRDC/DoD via MTEC-20-12-Diagnostics-023; Army/Air Force contract FA8702-15-D-0001 through MIT Lincoln Laboratory; #StartSmall foundation; "Oura Health Oy provided 1400 pieces of hardware and financial support in the form of a sponsored research contract." Mason and Smarr are co-inventors on Oura-filed patents; Smarr "has received remuneration for consulting work from, and has a financial interest in Oura Ring Inc."; Epel received loaned equipment from Oura.
- Population/setting/country: existing Oura owners "distributed globally" (Sites, p.9) plus frontline healthcare workers at 20 named US sites (UCSF, ZSFG, Stanford, Santa Clara Valley, Northwestern, BIDMC, Stony Brook, Weill Cornell, NYP Queens, NYP Brooklyn Methodist, Miami, UTSW, Tufts, Jamaica Hospital, UCLA, Boston Medical Center, Kaiser San Diego, FAU, American Medical Response). Age/sex/race in Table 1 (p.4): 60% male, 82% non-Hispanic white, 85% associate degree or higher, 13% frontline workers.
- Enrolled to analysed (Study overview, p.2 and Fig 1): 73,399 responded to the in-app invitation; 62,139 met all inclusion criteria; 3,180 healthcare workers enrolled separately (target 3,400, Methods p.9); pool 65,319; 242 withdrew; 1,924 "did not engage sufficiently with study activities to be included in analyses (i.e., completed the baseline survey, but did not complete any of the daily or monthly follow up surveys)"; 63,153 analysed. Of these, 704 self-reported possible COVID-19, 306 had reliable lab-confirmed diagnoses after removing 11 test-ambiguous and 7 survey-ambiguous cases, 73 met data-completeness criteria for training, 10 further for antibody-confirmed validation. Antibody DBS kits mailed to 10,021; 3,664 had antibody results at case-definition time (p.10).
- Duration: recruitment 19 March to 23 September 2020; consent initially to 30 August 2020, re-consent offered to extend to 30 November 2020 (p.9).
- Deployment model: mixed. BYOD for existing Oura owners; study-provisioned rings mailed to HCWs after sizing ("We mailed sites recruitment materials, including study flyers and Oura Ring sizing kits, which contained plastic rings for healthcare workers to try on to determine their size", p.9). "We did not compensate participants for participation" (p.9).
- Custom integration: none beyond Oura's in-app data-sharing consent and Qualtrics surveys linked from a daily in-app message ("Participants were presented with a daily in-app message that linked to a brief survey", p.9). Vendor cooperation (in-app invitation, in-app survey prompt) is itself a deployment feature worth flagging as not reproducible without Oura's participation.
- Recruitment method: vendor in-app invitation ("sending them invitations within the Oura App on their smartphones", p.9), no ceiling; HCWs via site leadership and per-site IRB.
- Retention definition and rate: not defined as such. Withdrawal 242/65,319 (0.37%); non-engagement 1,924/65,319 (2.9%).
- Attrition reasons: not reported beyond withdrew / did not engage.
- Data completeness: no cohort-level figure. Case-level selection thresholds (p.12): "Oura data available on at least 20 days (consecutive or not) that would be usable as baseline (at least 17 days prior to DX) and had at least 7 days of data prior to DX and 14 days following DX", plus "complete heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature data"; 73 of 306 confirmed cases (23.9%) met these. Antibody-kit prioritisation used "minimum 100,000 observations and maximum 2 day gap" as a quality bar (p.9). Baseline normalisation fell back to 49-day windows "In several cases, missing data precluded the use of the full 21 days" (p.12). Shiba's reading of this: all TemPredict papers "commented on the need for participant exclusion due to missing data, which could be due to adherence issues or, at the time, issues with ensuring data capture."
- Wear-time definition: non-wear inferred, not measured: "comparing MET values against a fixed threshold of 0.5. We treated values below this threshold as non-wear and discarded both MET and dermal temperature measurements during these non-wear periods" (p.12). PPG-derived HR/HRV/RR "are only available during periods of sleep" (p.10), so the physiological streams are effectively night-only.
- OS breakdown: not reported (Oura app is on both stores).
- Documented technical failure modes: charging artefacts ("elevated temperature readings, saved while an Oura Ring is charging", p.12); survey-report conflicts requiring manual follow-up with 113 participants (p.11); test-type questions absent from the daily survey in the early weeks (p.11). Vendor policy: "Oura's data use policy does not permit us to make the data available to third parties without approval" and classifier code "limited by the Department of Defense" (Data availability, p.13).
- Authors' feasibility conclusions: none on deployment; real-time deployment feasibility named as future work.
- NOT reported: adherence or wear-time distribution across the 63,153; how many of the 3,180 provisioned HCWs actually paired and shared data; ring loss/replacement; sync or upload failures; daily-survey response rate at cohort level; time from consent to ring receipt for HCWs; battery burden; app-version issues; any per-site enrolment counts.

These are worth a line in Module 1's Oura profile, not a Module 3 entry.

## 4. Shiba et al. 2023, Cureus

- File: `txt/2023-shiba-cureus-adherence-oura-covid-healthcare-workers.txt`
- First author (byline): Steven K. Shiba. Year 2023. Journal: Cureus 15(9):e45362. DOI 10.7759/cureus.45362. PMCID: not in PDF.
- Device actually deployed (Data collection, p.3): "all participants wore the Oura Gen 2 ring device on a finger of their choosing. The Oura Ring Gen 2 collects the data and transfers it to its standard commercial mobile app (Google Play or Apple App Stores) via Bluetooth, where it is then uploaded to a cloud storage interface managed by Oura Health Oy." Study staff monitored via "a secure Oura Teams account." Surveys via UCSF Qualtrics linked from the Oura app; data moved UCSF to FAU into REDCap. Matches filename.
- Verdict: INCLUDE. Adherence to the wearable and to the daily survey is the primary outcome, with definitions, subgroup and phase breakdowns, and an exclusion count with reasons. Multi-week (minimum 8 weeks), provisioned devices, clinical-staff population under pandemic conditions.
- Cohort relationship: FAU substudy of TemPredict (see Mason above). No existing Module 3 profile.

Extraction

- Design: "mixed-methods observational study" (Abstract); two enrolment phases as the pandemic evolved; per-protocol analysis.
- Funding/COI (Disclosures p.7-8): DoD/USAMRDC MTEC-20-12-COVID19-D.-023; AFOSR via MIT Lincoln Laboratory FA8702-15-D-0001; #StartSmall foundation 7029991; Oura Health Oy 134650; Florida Blue Foundation charitable contribution. "Oura Health Oy provided a portion of the wearable devices used in the study." Mason and Smarr: Oura patents, consulting fees (stated as not during data collection); Smarr holds Oura stock.
- Population/setting/country: FAU-affiliated healthcare professionals (core faculty, affiliate hospital consortium faculty, student health professionals), resident physicians, and medical students "working at multiple clinical sites in South Florida" (p.2), USA. Analysed 91: 35 HCP, 39 residents, 17 medical students; 59% female; mean age 36 +/- 13.
- Enrolled to analysed (p.2-3): 100 enrolled; 9 excluded, "for either withdrawing their consent/participation from the study (N=8) or losing the provided ring (N=1)"; 91 in the wearable analysis. "An additional eight participants' symptom survey data could not be retrieved, but these participants were included in the wearable adherence portion" so survey adherence N = 83 (Figure 2 legend: females 49, males 34, HCP 32, residents 35, students 16). Phase I: 39 enrolled; Phase II: 71, "including 19 participants who had continued their participation from Phase I" (p.3). Phase I survey N = 35, Phase II survey N = 67 (Figure 1 legend).
- Duration: "Participants were enrolled for a minimum of eight weeks" (p.2). Exact calendar dates and per-person enrolment length not reported; adherence is normalised to days enrolled.
- Deployment model: study-provisioned rings, sized in person after a virtual onboarding: "participants were sent study information electronically, followed by a virtual meeting where the protocol was thoroughly discussed, and baseline demographic information was collected. The consented participants were then met in person to retrieve an appropriately sized ring ... Training was administered on the proper use and set-up of the device, including the smart phone application" (p.2). Participants used their own smartphone (inclusion: "owned a smartphone").
- Custom integration: none; vendor app plus Oura Teams for staff monitoring, Qualtrics for surveys, REDCap for storage. Data flowed UCSF to FAU by "a secure and encrypted data transfer process" (p.3).
- Recruitment method: "recruited by multiple modalities" (p.2), not itemised; required co-enrolment in the UCSF TemPredict Study. Phase I limited to HCPs and residents in hospital settings "Due to safety concerns"; Phase II added medical students.
- Retention: 91/100 (91%) retained for analysis; 19/39 (48.7%) of Phase I participants opted to continue into Phase II.
- Attrition reasons: withdrawal (8), lost provided ring (1). Survey data unretrievable for 8 (reason not given).
- Adherence definitions (Outcomes, p.3): wearable adherence = "the percentage of nights enrolled in the study that sleep was recorded"; survey adherence = "the percentage of days in which a survey response was received relative to the total number of days each participant was enrolled." Important instrument caveat: "At the time of this study, sleep was only recorded if four hours of consecutive sleep were detected while the participant wore the ring due to the nature of the programming in the Oura Ring" (p.3), so the wear metric is really "nights with >= 4 h consecutive detected sleep" and undercounts short-sleep nights (relevant for residents).
- Adherence results (p.4-5): overall wearable 87.8 +/- 11.6% of nights vs survey 63.8 +/- 27.4% of days (t(172)=7.40, p<0.001, d=1.16). Phase I wearable 85.9 +/- 12.1%, Phase II 89.7 +/- 10.5% (ns). Survey Phase I 43.1 +/- 27.1% vs Phase II 74.7 +/- 21.3% (p<0.001, d=1.35). Females wearable 88.9 +/- 10.8%, survey 67.9 +/- 26.8%; males 86.0 +/- 12.5% and 57.9 +/- 27.1% (sex differences ns). HCP wearable 92.4 +/- 8.2%, survey 71.9 +/- 23.8%; medical students 89.6 +/- 9.9% and 78.1 +/- 13.4%; residents 82.8 +/- 12.9% and 49.8 +/- 28.7%. HCP vs residents significant on both (wearable p<0.001, d=0.88; survey p<0.005, d=0.83); residents vs students significant on survey only (p<0.001, d=-1.14).
- Adherence support: "Study staff monitored participant adherence and sent frequent reminders about the protocol" (p.2); staff "had continuous access to Oura Ring data for each participant located on the cloud interface through a secure Oura Teams account" (p.3). Reminder frequency and channel not specified.
- Wear-time definition: nights with recorded sleep (>= 4 h consecutive), not hours/day. Participants "were required to wear the Oura Ring each night while sleeping but were encouraged to wear it up to 24 hours/day" (p.2).
- OS breakdown: not reported.
- Documented technical failure modes: Oura firmware's 4-hour sleep-detection threshold as an adherence-measurement artefact (p.3); one lost ring; eight participants' survey data "could not be retrieved" (cross-institution data transfer gap, cause unstated). The Discussion (p.6) attributes missing data in the parent study partly to "issues with ensuring data capture" at the time.
- Authors' feasibility conclusions (p.5-6): "Overall, participants showed excellent adherence to both"; wearable adherence exceeded survey adherence "in every comparative analysis"; high adherence attributed to "emphasizing the importance of the study to participants, creating an overall environment of collaboration between researchers and participants, providing frequent feedback, and taking advantage of the ease of use of this wearable device"; lower survey adherence attributed to time cost; residents' lower adherence attributed to "increased stress and disrupted work schedules"; Phase II survey improvement possibly demographic mix or heightened concern. Recommendation: "wearable devices are likely a more efficacious tool in clinical practice and public health policy" than daily surveys; results "can guide the development of adherence strategies."
- NOT reported: calendar dates of the phases; per-participant enrolment length (only "minimum eight weeks"); distribution of adherence (only mean +/- SD); daytime wear; ring charging burden; any sync/app failures; time to first data after ring handout; whether the 4-hour rule was later changed; recruitment funnel above the 100 enrolled (approached, screened, declined); whether reminder intensity differed by phase; number of rings supplied by Oura vs purchased.

Funding: "funded by Oura Ring Inc."; Robbins on Oura Medical Advisory Board with consulting fees.
- Existing Module 3 profile: none.

## 8. Hirten et al. 2025, Gastroenterology (IBD Forecast Study)

- File: `txt/2025-hirten-gastroenterology-ibd-flares-wearables.txt`
- First author (byline): Robert P. Hirten. Year 2025. Journal: Gastroenterology 168(5):939-951.e5. DOI 10.1053/j.gastro.2024.12.024. PMCID: not printed (PDF is the HHS author manuscript, "available in PMC 2026 May 01").
- Devices/platform actually deployed (Methods p.4): "A participant could use their own Apple Watch, Fitbit, or Oura Ring, or be provided with an Apple Watch or Fitbit for the duration of the study." "Participants downloaded ehive, our custom digital research platform, to their smartphones, self-verified inclusion and exclusion criteria and signed the electronic study consent. Participants linked their wearable device or requested a wearable device from the study team." Results p.7: "255 participants wore an Apple Watch, 53 participants wore a Fitbit while 16 participants wore an Oura Ring, as some subjects used 2 types of devices over the study period ... In total, 120 devices were loaned to participants (107 Apple Watches, 13 Fitbits)." ehive is a Mount Sinai in-house app (ref 18), not a Module 2 platform; the Module 1 hook is Apple Watch (`apple-watch-healthkit`), Fitbit (`fitbit-google`) and Oura. Filename says "wearables", consistent.
- Verdict: BORDERLINE-include. For: a fully remote, nationwide (36 states), open-ended multi-device deployment with a mixed BYOD/loaner model, a stated compliance definition, a reminder rule, mean follow-up of 213 days, and an explicit statement about missing-data mechanism. Against: the actual usage/adherence statistics are deferred to "Supplemental Table 1" which is not in the PDF; no recruitment funnel above the 309; no attrition or retention rate; no technical failure modes beyond the generic "technological and behavioral factors, not easily determined." Profile would carry several "not reported in main text; see supplement" cells. Extraction

- Design: "prospective exploratory observational cohort study enrolling individuals throughout the United States" (p.4). Open December 2021 to June 2023.
- Funding/COI (p.13): NIDDK K23DK129835 (Hirten); philanthropic support (Jenny Steingart). Hirten consults for BMS; Keefer, Fayad and Sands list extensive pharma consulting; no device-vendor funding stated. Apple/Fitbit/Oura not listed as funders or hardware donors.
- Population/setting/country: adults with CD (196) or UC (113) on IBD medication, USA, remote; mean age 39.9 (SD 14.4); 67.3% female; 90.0% white (Table 1). Exclusions include pacemaker/defibrillator and HR-affecting medications (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, benzodiazepines).
- Enrolled to analysed: "A total of 309 participants consented and contributed both wearable device and survey data" (p.7). No count of consented-but-never-contributed, screened, or approached. Device split 255 / 53 / 16 with overlap (sums to 324 > 309).
- Duration: "The mean duration of follow up was 213 (SD 156) days" (p.7); "Participants were able to contribute for as long as they would like" (p.4). No median, range, or per-device breakdown.
- Deployment model: BYOD for those who owned a device; loaners for 120 (107 Apple Watch, 13 Fitbit; no Oura loaned). "No compensation was provided" (p.4). Consent, eligibility self-verification, device linking, and surveys all in-app.
- Custom integration: ehive (Mount Sinai custom research platform, JMIR Res Protoc 2023;12:e49204) aggregating Apple Watch, Fitbit and Oura data plus daily PRO-2 surveys and self-reported labs; EHR pull for labs if followed at Mount Sinai (p.5). Device type was a fixed effect in models. Cross-device metric harmonisation: Apple Watch HRV is SDNN, Fitbit and Oura HRV is RMSSD; SpO2 Apple Watch Series 6+ only (p.4-5).
- Recruitment method: "nationally recruited via social media advertising, study flyers, a study website, and an email about the study to individuals with IBD who visited the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Center at Mount Sinai Hospital" (p.4). Yield per channel not reported.
- Retention definition and rate: not reported.
- Attrition reasons: not reported.
- Adherence/completeness: wear recommendation "a minimum of 8 hours per day" (p.4); reminder rule "Participants who used their wearable device or answered daily surveys less than 4 days per week were sent a smart phone push notification" (p.4); "a priori definition of study compliance, which asked participants to answer a minimum of 4 surveys per 7-day period" (p.5); symptomatic-flare classification required >= 4 surveys per 7-day window, chosen after a sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Figure 1, not in PDF). Actual usage: "Mean wearable device usage statistics are presented in Supplemental Table 1" (p.7), not in PDF. Missing-data handling (p.5-6): baseline covariates imputed (Hmisc aregImpute); "Longitudinal wearable measures were not imputed"; MAR assumed; "Factors leading to missing wearable data are dictated by technological and behavioral factors, not easily determined." A sensitivity analysis found nocturnal-only vs 24-h RHR made no difference to outcomes ("results not presented", p.6). Prediction analyses restricted to Apple Watch users (p.9).
- Wear-time definition: not reported beyond the 8 h/day recommendation.
- OS breakdown: not reported (Apple Watch implies iOS for 255; Fitbit/Oura users' phone OS not stated).
- Documented technical failure modes: none specific. The authors note "physical activity measurements are prone to bias due to device non-adherence" (p.11) and that "digital study cohorts have been shown to differ ... including in composition and adherence" (p.12).
- Authors' feasibility conclusions: "demonstrates the feasibility of identifying and predicting IBD flares using non-invasive commonly used wearable technologies" (p.10) and "suggesting their feasibility to monitor and predict IBD activity" (Abstract). Feasibility is asserted from the analytic result, not from adherence data. Stated limitations are about lab timing, flare definitions, imbalanced outcomes, uncontrolled medication, and digital-cohort bias.
- NOT reported (main text): screened/approached/declined counts; withdrawals and reasons; per-device wear-days or hours; survey response rate; proportion meeting the 4-surveys/week rule; loaner return/loss; time-to-first-data; sync failures between vendor clouds and ehive; whether Oura users had a subscription; iOS/Android split; how many participants switched devices and why.
