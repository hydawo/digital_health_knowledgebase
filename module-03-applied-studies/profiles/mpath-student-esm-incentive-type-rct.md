## Summary

This is an experiment about one design lever. Every arm used the same m-Path protocol, nine beeps a day for 14 days. Only the payment structure differed.

Payment per beep raised full compliance to 90.81%. The fixed-payment arm reached 86.64%. The fixed-payment-plus-feedback arm reached 86.53% (86.38% including the two participants excluded from the model). Both comparisons with the per-beep arm were significant (p<.001). The two fixed arms did not differ (p=.809).

Nothing else moved. Incentive type had no effect on careless responding, socially desirable responding, response latency, momentary or retrospective burden, or motivation. The effect on compliance did not change over the 14 days.

Per-beep payment was also cheaper. The per-beep arm was paid a mean of about 57.21 euros for the ESM period, 5.79 euros less than the fixed arms.

## Instrumentation and Deployment Model

Bring your own device. Owning a smartphone was an inclusion criterion. The preprint does not report the iOS and Android split.

m-Path sent nine signal-contingent, semi-random beeps a day between 9:00 and 22:30. The day was split into nine 75-minute windows, one beep per window, each followed by 15 minutes with no beep. Each questionnaire was available for 15 minutes. It held 24 to 28 items depending on branching. Four beeps carried an attention-check item. Four others carried two extra questions about the response process.

The ESM period usually began the day after the baseline lab session. The feedback arm was shown an example feedback report at baseline. All participants could receive a feedback report at the end.

## Recruitment and Retention

Recruitment used social media and campus advertisements followed by a screening questionnaire. The numbers screened and found ineligible are not reported.

Retention was 100%. The authors write that "no participants dropped out of the study, and we were not aware of any technical problems during it."

## Data Completeness and Technical Issues

Full compliance was defined per beep as all 20 mandatory items completed. Partial compliance was the proportion of those items completed. Partially compliant responses were rare, 163 of them (0.67%) from 79 participants. Partial compliance was therefore not modelled.

Only 1.56% of participants failed the attention check at least once. The inconsistency index could not be computed because no affect-item pair reached the required within-person correlation.

No technical failures are reported.

## Feasibility Findings

The authors recommend payment per beep for motivated, non-clinical student samples. They argue it boosts data quantity by more than four points without costing data quality. They add that it is cheaper when the maximum payout is fixed.

They found no support for personalised feedback as a compliance lever. They note that almost all participants in the other two arms chose to receive their feedback report anyway.

They flag a likely ceiling effect. Compliance was high in every arm, so the result may not transfer to clinical samples with lower baseline compliance.

## Relevance to Future Study Design

This is the cleanest evidence in the module on payment structure for EMA. It isolates incentive type in a sample of 192 with no attrition.

The effect is modest in absolute terms. A gain of just over four points on a base near 87% is worth having in a power calculation. It is not a fix for a struggling protocol.

Feedback reports did not change behaviour here. A team adding feedback for engagement reasons should not expect it to raise compliance in a student sample.

Results come from a student sample with a 15-minute response window. Clinical populations may respond differently, and the authors say so.

## Evidence Confidence

Corroborated for the headline compliance result, since the preprint and the published abstract agree on 192 students, 14 days, nine beeps a day and a compliance advantage for per-beep payment.
