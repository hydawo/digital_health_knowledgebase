## Summary

This paper is about who does not take part and who stops. Both questions are usually invisible in app studies. It is the module's clearest account of reasons for non-participation in a clinical BYOD app study.

Of 88 outpatients approached in person, 31 declined. Of 57 who started, 17 dropped out (29.8%). Every dropout was interviewed.

App-related reasons dominated dropout. Fourteen of 17 gave at least one. Unnoticed push messages (n=4) and annoying push messages (n=3) were both named.

Early mood reporting separated dropouts from completers. Dropouts reported mood significantly less often in the first five days (U=48.00, p<.001, r=0.61).

## Instrumentation and Deployment Model

The paper states that the authors used "an internet platform called m-Path developed by KU Leuven (Belgium)" to build the app. Registration needed no email, phone number or name. Smartphone-to-server traffic is described as end-to-end encrypted.

Staff installed the app on each patient's own phone at the consent meeting. Inclusion required a smartphone that supported the app and internet access. Push messages prompted three mood questionnaires a day, each answerable once in a six-hour window. The app also gave awards.

Participants who agreed got a follow-up call in week one. The final questionnaires were prompted by push message and a phone call.

OS breakdown is not reported.

Dropouts gave a mean 2.82 reasons each. One dropout named a smartphone change. One named the app icon as too inconspicuous. No one named lack of financial compensation as a reason for declining.

Dropouts and completers did not differ significantly on any sociodemographic, clinical or app-related variable.

## Data Completeness and Technical Issues

Three of 17 dropouts left no mood data. Two had deleted all their in-app data themselves. One answered only the baseline questionnaire.

Among the 14 dropouts with data, mood reports came from a mean 6.33 people per day over days 1 to 12. After day 12, fewer than half of them (6) reported at all. The authors therefore analysed only days 1 to 12.

Among completers, 38 of 40 had complete data sets. A mean 34.08 of those 38 reported mood per day.

No participant in either group reported a problem with the app at the week-one follow-up call. One non-participant reported an error during installation.

## Feasibility Findings

The authors conclude that motivation, app-related aspects, lack of interest in app-based offers and health complaints drive non-participation and dropout. They recommend more incentives (personalised feedback, gamification, more push notifications), shorter questionnaires, several installation appointments, and user involvement in app design.

They propose using mood reports in the first days as an early warning of dropout.

## Relevance to Future Study Design

Install time is a recruitment barrier in itself. Five of 29 decliners named no time for installation, even with staff installing the app on site.

Push notifications cut both ways. Four dropouts did not notice them, and three found them annoying.

Watch reporting in the first five days. It separated dropouts from completers before the one-week dropout definition was met.

Participant-side data deletion is possible on this deployment. Two dropouts deleted all their own data, so their records could not be analysed.
